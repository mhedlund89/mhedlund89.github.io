Ingredient Hub

A daily-updating database of restaurant and packaged-food ingredients, built so a person avoiding an allergen can see what a brand has actually said — and, just as importantly, where a brand has said nothing at all.

Why this document exists now

The original goal was to find a public API carrying ingredients for the big American restaurant and beverage companies. That API does not exist. No brand among McDonald's, Yum! Brands, Darden, Brinker, Dine Brands, Cracker Barrel, Coca-Cola or PepsiCo publishes ingredient data through a developer interface. What exists is web pages, PDFs, and one third-party platform.

So rather than plan against assumptions, we built the smallest real thing — one brand, end to end — and measured what came back. This PRD is written against that measurement. Every number below is from a live ingestion run, not an estimate.

The finding that reshaped the plan

Measured, not assumed

58% of McDonald's menu items carry no allergen declaration from the brand at all. That includes the plain Hamburger and the McRib. Across all 277 items there are zero peanut declarations and zero tree-nut declarations — not because the food is free of them, but because the brand's Contains: statements are patchy rather than systematic.

This matters more than any architectural decision in the project. The clean path — read the brand's own FDA-style Contains: declaration and report it — works beautifully for the 42% of items that have one. For the rest, every allergen comes back not_stated, and an item rendered as “no allergens declared” reads to a frightened parent as “no allergens.”

The McDonald's Chocolate Chip Cookie is the clearest example. Its ingredient list names bleached wheat flour, egg, milkfat, whey and soy lecithin. It carries no Contains: statement. The brand-assertion path finds nothing.

Consequence for the roadmap: ingredient-text inference was originally a later phase, a nice-to-have refinement on top of brand declarations. The measurement moves it to load-bearing. Without it, the product is silent on the majority of items.

Limitation of this evidence — n = 1 brand

Every number in this document comes from McDonald's, and McDonald's sits in Family A — the family with the weakest allergen data by construction. Family B sources publish matrices, which are complete by design: every item gets a row and every allergen a column, so a Darden guide cannot be 58% silent the way a free-text statement can.

So the 58% gap may be a Family A problem rather than a universal one, and the decision to promote inference ahead of everything else rests on a single brand from the worst-case family. Before committing to that ordering, the cheap check is to pull one Darden PDF and one EveryBite payload and measure their declaration coverage directly. If Family B and C are near-complete, the sequencing argument weakens considerably and breadth beats depth.

Who this is for

One primary user: a person who must avoid a specific allergen, or who shops and orders for someone who does. They arrive with a narrow, urgent question — “does this item have milk in it?” — and they need an answer they can act on, plus an honest signal when no answer exists.

A secondary user is the maintainer reviewing what changed today: which formulations moved, which parsers broke, which brands went dark. This user keeps the first user safe, so the review tooling is a product surface, not an internal admin page.

The four states

The core information design of the product. There are deliberately only four, and none of them means “safe.”

present Listed in an ingredient statement, or marked in a brand's allergen matrix. The strongest signal we can carry.
ambiguous A precautionary “may contain” warning, or wording we could not resolve. Not a confirmed ingredient — calling it present would put words in the brand's mouth — and emphatically not safe. Renders as “do not assume safe.”
absent_per_brand The brand affirmatively states this is not an ingredient. Available only from allergen matrices and platform data. Speaks to ingredients only — it says nothing about shared fryers.
not_stated No source we hold addresses this allergen for this item. The majority case today. Carries no implication of absence whatsoever.

There is no plain absent value, and the database schema physically cannot store one — a CHECK constraint rejects it. That rule lives in the schema rather than in validation code specifically so a careless future caller cannot route around it.

One rule, applied twice

Two separate problems turned out to have the same answer, which is the most useful thing to come out of reviewing v0.1.

Across sources: Cracker Barrel will arrive via both the EveryBite platform and its own published guide. Those two records can disagree. Across components: a Big Mac is a bun, a patty, a sauce and a slice of cheese, each with its own allergen profile.

Both resolve by conservative union. A present anywhere — any source, any component — makes the item present. An absent_per_brand can never overturn a present; at most it can fill a gap where nothing else has spoken. One rule covers both, and it fails in the direction of over-warning, which is the only survivable direction.

This makes the disagreement itself valuable: whenever two sources conflict, the union keeps the user safe and the conflict raises a review flag pointing at a likely parser bug. The safety mechanism and the quality mechanism are the same mechanism.

What conservative union does not solve

Item identity across sources is still unmodelled. item_key is currently source_id:slug(name) — source-scoped — so the same food arriving from two brands' feeds produces two unrelated records with no link between them. Until an entity-resolution layer exists, the union above can only be applied within a source. Cross-source union is blocked on that work, and it must land before EveryBite (Phase 3) introduces the first real overlap.

System plan

Five stages. Raw bytes are captured before anything is parsed, so a parser that breaks still leaves behind the document that broke it.

StageModuleStatusResponsibility
1 · fetchfetcher.jsbuiltrobots.txt per host, per-host rate limit, identifying User-Agent, 403/429 → stop permanently rather than retry
2 · capturesnapshot.jsbuiltImmutable content-addressed store of raw bytes. Nothing overwrites; identical bytes collapse to one path

Three source families

The brands publish different shapes of data, not just different content. This is the single most important structural fact about the domain.

FamilyBrandsShapeAllergen determination
A McDonald's, Taco Bell, KFC, Pizza Hut Free-text ingredient statements Brand Contains: where present (42%); ours by inference otherwise
B Darden (8 brands), Brinker (2) PDF item × allergen grids Brand's own, including negative assertions. No ingredient detail
C EveryBite platform (~54 chains incl. Cracker Barrel, TGI Fridays) Structured platform data Brand's own, at individual-ingredient granularity. Best available source

Family B carries allergens without ingredients; Family A carries ingredients from which allergens must be inferred; Family C carries both. Neither A nor B alone answers every question, which is why the canonical schema records which family each claim came from alongside the claim itself.

FR-7Changes enter a review queue rather than auto-publishing; the same pending issue is never queued twiceA diff may mean a reformulation or a broken parser. Duplicates would bury real findings

FR-10Where sources disagree, the resolved state is the conservative union: a present from any source always beats an absent_per_brand from any other. Disagreement additionally raises a review flagOver-warning is recoverable; under-warning is not. Also gives us a free signal for finding bad parsers
FR-11Review gating is asymmetric by safety direction. A change that adds or escalates an allergen publishes immediately; a change that removes or downgrades one blocks on human reviewDelaying a new warning endangers users. FR-12Each run reconciles the seen-set. Items no longer published are tombstoned — withdrawn from results, history retainedServing a discontinued formulation as current is a live hazard. Nothing detects this today
FR-13Allergen claims are held per component. An item's claim is the conservative union of its components. Sources without component data get one implicit whole-item componentThe real question is usually about a modified order. Uniform modelling keeps matrix sources on the same code path
FR-14Claims can be re-derived from stored snapshots without re-fetching. Re-derivations are classified as parser_change, distinct from source changesOtherwise one dictionary tweak floods the review queue with hundreds of false “the brand changed something” entries
FR-15Inference accuracy is measured against a hand-labelled gold set drawn from items that lack brand declarationsScoring against the 42% that have declarations tests inference on exactly the items brands chose to label — a biased sample that proves nothing about the 58%
FR-16A run that completes with zero items, or a source silent for more than 48 hours, raises an operator alertA quietly broken adapter is indistinguishable from a brand with no changes
FR-17Allergen state is never conveyed by colour alone, and the interface is fully operable by screen reader and keyboardPeople make health decisions here; colour-blind and low-vision users are not an edge case

Explicitly not building

✕“Safe to eat” verdicts or a gluten-free/allergen-free filter. Cross-contact is invisible to every source we have. Cracker Barrel's own guidance is blunter than anything we would write: fried and grilled items should not be consumed if you have a food allergy.
✕Nutrition and calorie tracking. Abundantly served by free sources already. Adding it dilutes the one thing this does that nothing else does.
✕Circumventing bot protection. Applebee's returns 403 to a plain request, so Dine Brands ships as unsupported. Blocks are respected, not routed around.
✕Real-time or per-location accuracy. Formulations change quarterly; regional and limited-time items are frequently undocumented. Daily change detection, not live truth.

Success measures

  • Declaration coverage: share of items with at least one resolved allergen claim. Baseline 42% today, from brand declarations alone. Phase 1 should move this decisively; the honest target is coverage, not a coverage percentage chosen in advance.
  • Provenance completeness: 100% of claims trace to a retrievable snapshot and a working public URL. Currently met.
  • Review latency: a flagged change is resolved before it is presented as current — the queue should not grow monotonically.
  • Inference agreement: where both a brand declaration and our inference exist, they agree. Disagreements are the highest-value bug signal in the system.
  • Zero false all-clears: no item ever renders as having no allergens when its own ingredient text names one. This is the only metric with a hard target.

Open risks

  • Inference is now safety-critical. Promoting it to Phase 1 means our own parsing, not the brand's declaration, becomes the answer for most items. That shifts real liability onto our dictionary and demands adversarial testing for derivative names — whey, casein, semolina, albumin, tahini.
  • Zero peanut and tree-nut declarations across 277 items is a strong signal that brand declarations cannot be trusted as complete, even where present. Absence of a declaration is not evidence, and now we have measured proof.
  • Family B gives allergens without ingredients. Darden and Brinker items will never answer non-allergen dietary questions. This is a permanent shape difference, not a coverage gap to close.
  • EveryBite is unsanctioned pending reply and may change or withdraw access at any time. Nothing may depend on it.
  • Adapters are permanently maintenance-bearing. Chains restructure pages and revise PDFs at will. 17 of 317 detail fetches already failed on the first live run.
  • Component data is only available from Family A. Darden and Brinker publish item-level matrices with no component breakdown, so customization questions will be answerable for QSR items and unanswerable for casual dining. That asymmetry is a property of the sources, not something more engineering can fix, and the interface must not imply otherwise.
  • Conservative union will over-warn, by design. A single bad parse that emits a spurious present now propagates to the item and cannot be overridden by a brand's negative assertion. The disagreement flag is the mitigation, which makes review throughput a safety dependency rather than a nicety.
  • Asymmetric gating trusts the diff classifier. Auto-publishing allergen-adding changes means a parser that hallucinates allergens ships to users unreviewed. The classifier that decides “is this change safety-increasing?” becomes safety-critical code in its own right.
  • No liability position exists yet. There is no terms-of-use, no disclaimer surface, and no stated stance on what happens if someone is harmed after relying on this. For a tool in this domain that is a product gap, not a legal footnote, and it should be resolved before anything is exposed publicly.
  • Scope is US-only and undeclared. The McDonald's adapter hardcodes country=US&language=en. Formulations differ by market, so a non-US user reading this data would be misled. This needs to become an explicit non-goal or an explicit roadmap item.
  • The deepest limit is not technical. This dataset can support avoidance decisions. It cannot support safety clearance, and no amount of engineering changes that.
